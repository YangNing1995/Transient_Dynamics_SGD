\section{Supplementary empirical experiments}
\subsection{Experimental setup}

We utilized a fixed subset of the MNIST dataset \cite{lecun1998gradient}, containing a training set of $N=1,000$ samples (100 randomly selected images per class) and a test set of $N=200$ samples. Input images were flattened into vectors of dimension $d_\mathrm{in}=784$. The model architecture consisted of a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with two hidden layers of width 50 (architecture: $784 \to 50 \to 50 \to 10$), employing ReLU activations. The model parameters were optimized using standard Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) without momentum or weight decay to minimize the cross-entropy loss.

To isolate the stochasticity introduced by batch sampling, we strictly enforced a deterministic initialization. Before the first run, weights were generated using the Kaiming Normal (He) initialization scheme and biases were set to zero. This configuration ($\bm{\theta}_0$) was saved and reloaded for all independent realizations. We conducted a systematic grid search over batch sizes $B \in \{10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500\}$ and learning rates $\eta \in \{0.002, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1\}$. To ensure consistent convergence regimes, the total number of training iterations $T$ was scaled inversely with the learning rate according to $T = 100 / \eta$.

System trajectories were recorded at 101 evenly spaced time points $t \in [0, T]$. To analyze the landscape geometry efficiently, we tracked the specific weight matrix connecting the two hidden layers ($W^{(2)}$), treating it as a flattened parameter vector of dimension $d=2,500$. We computed the Hessian matrix $\mathbf{H}$ of the loss function restricted to this subspace at each checkpoint. Concurrently, we monitored functional performance (loss and accuracy) and tracked the indices of misclassified test samples to characterize sample-wise learning dynamics throughout the optimization process.

\subsection{Multi-valley landscape structure and Hessian spectrum}

Independent SGD runs initialized from the same deterministic starting point converge to multiple distinct low-loss endpoints that form separated clusters in parameter space. Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape_hessian}(a) visualizes this multi-valley structure by projecting the training trajectories for a representative subset of hyperparameter conditions onto the first two principal components of the collected endpoint weight vectors, with the vertical axis showing the training loss. Despite sharing the same initialization (star marker), trajectories under different noise conditions diverge and settle into distinct final clusters (open markers), confirming that the loss landscape contains multiple competing basins of attraction.

To quantify the functional dissimilarity between these endpoints, we computed the pairwise Jaccard similarity $\mathrm{Sim}_J$ (Eq.~2 of the main text) between the misclassified test sets of all converged endpoints across the full hyperparameter grid. The resulting similarity matrix, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape_hessian}(b), reveals a block-diagonal structure: endpoints trained under the same hyperparameter condition tend to be highly similar to one another, while endpoints from different conditions---particularly those differing in batch size---exhibit substantially lower similarity. This pattern indicates that the particular valley an SGD trajectory converges to is strongly influenced by the noise level during training.

We further verified that dissimilar endpoints are separated by loss barriers, confirming their assignment to distinct valleys. For each pair of converged endpoints $\bm{\theta}_A$ and $\bm{\theta}_B$, we evaluated the training loss along the linear interpolation path $\bm{\theta}(\alpha) = (1-\alpha)\bm{\theta}_A + \alpha\bm{\theta}_B$ for $\alpha \in [0,1]$ and recorded the barrier height $\Delta\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{barrier} = \max_\alpha \mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}(\alpha)) - \tfrac{1}{2}[\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_A) + \mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_B)]$. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape_hessian}(c) and (d), endpoint pairs with high Jaccard similarity ($\mathrm{Sim}_J = 0.95$) share a flat interpolation path with negligible barriers, indicating they reside in the same valley. In contrast, pairs with low similarity ($\mathrm{Sim}_J = 0.625$) are separated by a prominent loss barrier, confirming they occupy distinct valleys. The negative correlation between the barrier height and the Jaccard similarity across all sampled pairs (Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape_hessian}(e); $R^2 = 0.64$) further validates the Jaccard similarity as a reliable proxy for basin identity.

To investigate the local geometry of these valleys, we analyzed the Hessian spectrum at the optimization endpoints. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape_hessian}(f), the rank-ordered eigenvalue spectrum $\lambda_i(\mathbf{H})$ exhibits a rapid decay regardless of the batch size used during training. The vast majority of dimensions represent ``soft modes'' (near-zero curvature), implying that each solution lies on a manifold that is locally flat in most directions. To quantify this anisotropy, we calculated the fraction of the total curvature (trace of $\mathbf{H}$) captured by the top-10 eigenvalues for all converged realizations. The choice of $k=10$ is motivated by prior empirical analyses~\cite{sagunEmpiricalAnalysisHessian2018a}, which suggest that the Hessian spectrum of over-parameterized classification networks separates into a bulk of near-zero eigenvalues and a small set of large outliers roughly corresponding to the number of classes ($C=10$ for MNIST). As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape_hessian}(g), for most solutions these top 10 dimensions alone account for over 80\% of the total curvature, confirming that the optimization effectively confines the system to a low-dimensional active subspace governed by these principal directions.

\subsection{Convergence stability and geometric properties of solutions}

To define the stability boundaries of the optimization, we mapped the convergence probability ($P_\mathrm{conv}$) across the hyperparameter grid (Fig.~\ref{fig:solution_stats}(a)). We identified a ``high-noise'' instability regime characterized by large learning rates and small batch sizes (e.g., $\eta \ge 0.05, B \le 50$), where the model consistently failed to achieve 100\% training accuracy. These configurations correspond to the excluded gray cells in Fig.~2 of the main text. Subsequent analyses are restricted to the stable regime.

We next examined the cosine similarity between solutions to complement the endpoint diversity analysis in the main text (Fig.~2(b)). As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:solution_stats}(b), low-noise settings produce highly collinear solution vectors ($\langle \mathrm{Sim}_{\cos} \rangle \approx 1$), whereas increasing the noise level leads to nearly orthogonal solutions. This trend is consistent with the increase in the across-repeat RMS endpoint radius $r_\theta$ reported in Fig.~2(b) of the main text. The same ordering is reflected in the weight displacement metric $\langle \|\Delta\bm{\theta}\|_2 \rangle$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:solution_stats}(c)), confirming that higher noise drives the final solutions significantly further from the initialization point $\bm{\theta}_0$.

Finally, we recorded the functional performance metrics for all configurations. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:solution_stats}(d) and (e), all converged runs achieve perfect training accuracy and near-zero training loss. However, the test loss landscape (Fig.~\ref{fig:solution_stats}(f)) exhibits significant variation across the grid, with moderate-to-high noise settings yielding lower test loss values compared to the full-batch baseline, consistent with the flatness and test accuracy trends shown in Fig.~2(c) and~2(d) of the main text.

\subsection{Details of continuation training}

To probe the basin occupied by an SGD trajectory at a given time, we use a deterministic continuation protocol. At a selected checkpoint time $t_c$, we load the SGD weights and continue optimization using full-batch gradient descent (GD) on the entire training set. Because full-batch GD removes mini-batch stochasticity, it cannot escape local minima; the continuation therefore descends into the basin determined by the starting point. The resulting endpoint identifies the specific loss valley occupied by the SGD trajectory at time $t_c$.

As an illustrative example (Fig.~1 of the main text), we started from a reference SGD trajectory trained with $B=50$, $\eta=0.05$. At each of the 101 checkpoints (evenly spaced in $[0, T]$ where $T = 100/\eta = 2000$), we branched training and continued with full-batch GD at $\eta = 0.05$ for up to 2,000 additional iterations. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:continuation_details}(a--b) and (d--e), all continuation runs rapidly converge to near-zero training loss and 100\% training accuracy. Despite this uniform convergence, the quality of the accessed basins varies substantially. Figures~\ref{fig:continuation_details}(c) and (f) show that solutions branched at early times ($t_c < 100$) exhibit poor test accuracy, whereas performance improves sharply throughout the transient phase and saturates around the freezing time $t_f$. This behavior indicates that SGD actively transitions from low-quality to high-quality basins before becoming dynamically trapped. Figure~\ref{fig:continuation_details}(g) further reveals a strong positive correlation between test accuracy and the flatness metric $F$ ($R^2 = 0.77$), providing quantitative support for the conclusions drawn in Fig.~1(c) and~1(e) of the main text.

\subsection{Freezing time estimation and robustness}

For the systematic grid scan reported in the main text (Fig.~2), we employed a \emph{matched continuation} protocol: for each hyperparameter setting $(\eta, B)$, the continuation GD used the same learning rate $\eta$ as the SGD training trajectory, ensuring that the continuation dynamics probe the basin structure at the same effective gradient scale. Each continuation ran on the full training set with early stopping (training loss $< 5 \times 10^{-3}$, accuracy $= 100\%$, patience of 3 evaluations). Basin identity was assessed by comparing each continuation endpoint to a reference endpoint obtained from the final SGD checkpoint using the Jaccard similarity of their misclassified test sets (Eq.~2 of the main text), declaring two endpoints to be in the same basin when $\mathrm{Sim}_J \ge S_\mathrm{th}$ with $S_\mathrm{th} = 0.9$. To determine the freezing time $t_f$ (Eq.~3 of the main text) efficiently, we used a coarse-to-fine adaptive scan: starting from the reference checkpoint, we scanned backward through every 10th saved checkpoint and, upon finding a basin mismatch, refined the search by examining every checkpoint in that neighborhood. The freezing time was then identified as the earliest checkpoint after which all subsequent endpoints remained in the same basin as the reference.

To verify robustness, we repeated the analysis using stricter Jaccard thresholds, $S_{\mathrm{th}}=0.95$ and $0.99$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:freeze_time_robustness}(a,b)). Across these threshold choices, the qualitative dependence on hyperparameters remains unchanged: increasing SGD noise systematically prolongs the transient phase. We also compared the original Jaccard-based freezing time with the time at which the SGD trajectory first reaches $100\%$ training accuracy (Fig.~\ref{fig:freeze_time_robustness}(c)). These two times are not identical definitions: $100\%$ training accuracy only records when all training labels are classified correctly, whereas $t_f$ records when the continuation endpoint has become stable in basin identity. Nevertheless, in most hyperparameter conditions the Jaccard-based freezing time occurs no later than the time to perfect training accuracy, indicating that basin commitment typically precedes final training-set convergence.

We further compared the Jaccard-based freezing time with two alternative freezing definitions: a prediction agreement criterion, where two endpoints are assigned to the same basin when they agree on the class labels of $\geq 99\%$ of the test samples (Fig.~\ref{fig:freeze_time_robustness}(d)); and a simple loss proxy that defines the freezing time as the first iteration at which the training loss drops below $\mathcal{L}_c = 0.1$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:freeze_time_robustness}(e)). As shown in the scatter plot (Fig.~\ref{fig:freeze_time_robustness}(f)), both alternative definitions exhibit strong positive correlations with the Jaccard-based freezing time ($R^2 = 0.89$ for prediction agreement, $R^2 = 0.90$ for loss proxy), confirming that delayed freezing is an intrinsic dynamical feature of the optimization process rather than an artifact of the particular definition.

\subsection{Generalization to convolutional neural networks on CIFAR-10}

To demonstrate that our findings are not limited to simple fully connected networks or the MNIST dataset, we replicated the core experiments using a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) trained on the CIFAR-10 dataset \cite{krizhevsky2009learning}.

\subsubsection{Experimental setup}
We utilized the CIFAR-10 dataset, which consists of 60,000 $32 \times 32$ color images in 10 classes. The model architecture, denoted as SimpleCNN, consists of three convolutional blocks followed by a classifier. Each convolutional block comprises a 2D convolution layer ($3 \times 3$ kernel, padding 1), a ReLU activation, and a $2 \times 2$ MaxPool layer. The channel depths for the three layers are 32, 64, and 64, respectively. The feature maps are flattened into a vector of dimension 1,024 and fed into a classifier consisting of a hidden fully connected layer with 128 units and a final output layer ($1024 \to 128 \to 10$).

We conducted a grid search over batch sizes $B \in \{20, 50, 100, 200, 500\}$ and learning rates $\eta \in \{0.002, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05\}$. For each condition, 10 independent realizations were trained. To analyze the landscape geometry and solution diversity, we specifically focused on the weight matrix connecting the hidden layer to the output layer. This matrix corresponds to a parameter space of $d=1,280$ dimensions ($128 \times 10$).

\subsubsection{Results}
The results on CIFAR-10 corroborate the transient freezing mechanism presented in the main text. Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn_cifar10}(a) visualizes the optimization dynamics in the projected weight space. Similar to the MNIST results, trajectories do not converge to a single global minimum but instead form isolated clusters, confirming that the loss landscape consists of multiple distinct valleys. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn_cifar10}(b), the overall Jaccard similarity ($\mathrm{Sim}_J$) is substantially lower than that observed in the MNIST setting (Fig.~1(d) of the main text), suggesting that different local minima on the CIFAR-10 landscape correspond to functionally more distinct solutions.

Direct comparisons at a fixed learning rate ($\eta=0.005$) confirm that reducing batch size extends the transient training phase (Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn_cifar10}(c)) and enhances solution flatness (Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn_cifar10}(d)). The Hessian eigenvalue spectrum of the final solutions (Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn_cifar10}(e)) shows that smaller batch sizes produce flatter minima with systematically smaller leading eigenvalues, consistent with the flatness metric $F$.

The heatmap panels (Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn_cifar10}(f--i)) mirror the structure of Fig.~2 in the main text. Because the continuation-based freezing time estimation requires training a separate GD trajectory for every checkpoint of every repeat---a cost that scales with the larger dataset and deeper architecture---we adopted the loss proxy definition ($\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{train} \leq 0.1$) validated in Sec.~I~E (Fig.~\ref{fig:freeze_time_robustness}(e,\,f); $R^2 = 0.90$). As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn_cifar10}(f), the normalized mean freezing time $\eta\langle t_f \rangle$ increases systematically with SGD noise, consistent with the MNIST results. The same noise ordering is reflected in the across-repeat RMS endpoint radius $r_\theta$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn_cifar10}(g)), the mean final flatness $\langle F \rangle$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn_cifar10}(h)), and the best-of-repeats test accuracy $\max(\mathrm{Acc}_\mathrm{test})$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:cnn_cifar10}(i)).

\section{Supplementary information of the minimal two-valley model}
\subsection{Simulation settings}

The two-valley loss landscape $\mathcal{L}(x,y)$ is defined by Eq.~5 in the main text. The geometric properties of the landscape—specifically the bifurcation, valley depth, and flatness—are controlled by a set of fixed parameters. The values used in our simulations are listed in Table~\ref{tab:parameters}.

\centering

\begin{tabular}{ccl}
\hline

\hline

The SGD dynamics on this landscape were simulated using the discrete-time update rule given in Eq.~10 of the main text:
\begin{equation}
    \bm{\theta}_{t+1} = \bm{\theta}_t - \eta \left( \nabla \mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_t) + \bm{\xi}_t \right),
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ denotes the learning rate and $\bm{\theta}_t = (x_t, y_t)$ represents the parameters at iteration $t$. To capture the geometry-dependent nature of SGD noise, the stochastic term $\bm{\xi}_t$ was modeled as a zero-mean Gaussian vector with a covariance matrix proportional to the local curvature:
\begin{equation}
    \bm{\xi}_t \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(0, \mathbf{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_t)\bigr), \quad \text{with} \quad \mathbf{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_t) = 2\sigma \mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta}_t).
\end{equation}
Here, $\sigma$ represents the noise strength. In empirical simulations, we employed the positive semi-definite rectification of the Hessian $\mathbf{H}$ by clipping negative eigenvalues to zero. This ensures numerical stability while preserving the anisotropic diffusion characteristics driven by the valley geometry.

We explored the parameter space by varying both $\eta$ and $\sigma$ across 10 logarithmically spaced values within the range $[10^{-2}, 10^{-1}]$. For each hyperparameter configuration $(\eta, \sigma)$, we performed $2000$ independent trials, each spanning $2\times10^4$ iterations. To mitigate initialization bias towards either valley, starting positions were fixed at $y_0 = 5$, with $x_0$ alternating between $-0.01$ and $+0.01$ across trials.

We quantified the dynamics using two key metrics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Convergence probability ($P_\mathrm{flat}$):} The fraction of trajectories that converged to the flatter valley ($x > 0$) by the end of the simulation.
    \item \textbf{Freezing time ($t_f$):} The iteration index of the final sign change in the $x$-coordinate (i.e., the last crossing of the ridge at $x=0$).
\end{enumerate}
The resulting phase diagrams characterizing these dynamics are presented in Fig.~3(c) and~3(d) of the main text.

\subsection{Robustness under quadratic noise scaling}

Our analysis indicates that the selection of flat minima is driven by the anisotropic nature of SGD noise, where fluctuations are amplified in directions of high curvature. Consequently, the qualitative behavior of the system should not rely strictly on the linear proportionality $\mathbf{\Sigma} \propto \mathbf{H}$, provided that the noise strength remains monotonically increasing with curvature.

To validate this robustness, we performed simulations using the quadratic scaling $\mathbf{\Sigma} \propto \mathbf{H}^2$, as derived from the ``Random Shift Model''~\cite{yangStochasticGradientDescent2023}. The results, presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:toy_model_H2}, demonstrate that the convergence probability ($P_\mathrm{flat}$) and the freezing time ($t_f$) are qualitatively consistent with those obtained using the linear model. Specifically, increasing the learning rate $\eta$ or the noise strength $\sigma$ consistently biases the selection probability towards the flatter minimum (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy_model_H2}(a)) and prolongs the transient search phase (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy_model_H2}(b)). This confirms that our conclusions regarding stability and flatness selection are insensitive to the specific functional form of the geometry-dependent noise.
